A Little About Me.

I study astronomy, write fiction, and stop to look at birds.

Hi! I'm Lyra, an undergraduate student at the University of Virginia studying Astronomy and Computer Science, with a minor in Philosophy in progress. I'm curious about an unreasonable number of things, enjoy thinking through difficult questions, and have a habit of following interesting ideas further than I originally planned.

I've loved science for as long as I can remember. I grew up reading encyclopedias and popular science books, and spent much of my childhood watching documentaries on CCTV's science and documentary channels. When I was eight, I watched Stephen Hawking's Universe, which first sparked my fascination with astronomy and space exploration. In middle school, I discovered that mathematics and the physical sciences came naturally to me, especially physics. My physics teacher's encouragement gave me the confidence to take that interest further and begin thinking seriously about studying it.

Some of my strongest memories from those years involve the night sky. During a family trip to Arxan in Inner Mongolia, I visited Luming Lake and saw the Milky Way clearly for the first time, far from the light pollution of the city. I still remember the extraordinary number of stars. A few years later, in August after graduating from middle school, I watched the Perseid meteor shower in Hongcun, China. That was when I made up my mind to study astronomy.

In high school, I joined the school's observatory and eventually became its vice president and head of the observing department. I organized observing sessions for lunar eclipses, planetary conjunctions, and other astronomical events, taught students how to use telescopes, and helped arrange a guest lecture by an astronomer from Purple Mountain Observatory. I enjoyed having opportunities to share astronomy with other students, especially when I could introduce them to something they had never observed before.

At UVA, I began to experience what doing astronomy research actually involves. I've spent plenty of time trying to figure out why a simulation produced a strange result, checking calculations, revisiting assumptions, and sometimes finding that I need to approach the question differently. I enjoy being able to follow a question in so much detail, especially when the process leads me somewhere I hadn't expected.

My relationship with computer science developed a little differently. I first encountered programming in fifth grade, when I attended a summer camp and made a few simple puzzle games in Scratch. I went on to study computer science in high school and took the AP Computer Science Principles and Computer Science A exams. When I started taking CS courses at UVA, my reasons were mostly practical. I knew programming would be useful for astronomy, and as my coursework and research became more advanced, I found myself relying on it far more than I had anticipated.

But somewhere along the way, I also discovered how much I enjoyed building software itself. I love the process of starting with an idea, working out the logic behind it, and gradually turning it into something that actually functions. There is a particular satisfaction in watching something take shape through decisions, revisions, experiments, and the occasional very stubborn bug.

I find that same satisfaction in creative writing. Programming and writing demand different kinds of precision, yet both give me an extraordinary amount of freedom to create. I can begin with something that exists entirely in my imagination, decide how its pieces fit together, and keep working until I have made something that another person can experience. That freedom is one of the reasons I became particularly interested in software development and game design.

My interest in human–computer interaction grew alongside these experiences. I've always loved natural history, museums, and science education, and I often find myself asking two questions whenever I encounter something fascinating: What other questions does this raise? And how could I share what I've learned with someone else? Working on interface design and interactive prototypes made me increasingly interested in how people explore information, how design shapes their understanding, and how technology can make complex ideas more accessible. I'm particularly interested in bringing these questions into scientific exploration and public education.

Outside of academics, I write essays and fiction, take photographs, play indie games, watch documentaries, and spend a considerable amount of time observing birds and other wildlife. Around UVA, I'm particularly fond of the squirrels that get wonderfully round in autumn, the groundhog near my residence hall, the crows nesting downstairs, the great blue heron fishing at Dell Pond, and the red-tailed hawks circling overhead in the late afternoon. I enjoy watching common birds just as much as unusual ones. A sparrow going about its afternoon is already enough to make me happy. I love knowing that so much of the natural world carries on independently of human concerns, and spending time observing it always reminds me how much I love being in this world.

I'm also a feminist, an enthusiastic reader, and a fan of very large brunch omelets. My ideal free Saturday begins around lunchtime, includes some writing, a documentary, or an indie game, and ends with strength training and matcha Häagen-Dazs. I maintain that ice cream eaten after a workout has no calories, since it's cold. My scientific training has yet to convince me otherwise.

DESIGN NOTE / THIS WEBSITE

How I Made This Website

This is actually my first time designing and building a website! I started by figuring out what kind of visual style I liked, then spent some time experimenting with color palettes, fonts, and different ways of arranging text and images. I eventually settled on six colors, Times New Roman, and a minimalist layout with geometric sections and thin dividing lines. From there, I kept adjusting the spacing, proportions, and composition as I worked on each page.

I was also learning web development along the way, from understanding how HTML and CSS work to organizing the site's structure and deploying it through GitHub Pages. There's been quite a bit of trial and error, and I'm still figuring things out as I continue adding new projects and making changes to the site.

I've really enjoyed the whole process, especially getting to experiment with both the visual design and the technical side of building something from scratch.
