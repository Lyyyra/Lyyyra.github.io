01 / RESEARCH

The research question

Dust grains around stars are subject to several competing forces. Stellar gravity attracts them; radiation pressure acts outward; Poynting–Robertson drag gradually changes their orbital energy and angular momentum; and an electrically charged grain also responds to electric and magnetic fields. A stellar magnetic field may therefore change whether a small grain escapes, migrates inward, or remains in a confined region.

For my senior thesis with Professor Phil Arras, I investigate how these forces shape the trajectories of charged dust. I am particularly interested in the circumstances under which magnetic fields produce bounded, oscillatory motion and how those trajectories depend on grain size, charge, magnetic polarity, and the rotation of the stellar magnetic field.

02 / RESEARCH

Building the numerical model

I developed a Python simulation workflow using REBOUND for orbital integration, with additional force calculations for radiation pressure, Poynting–Robertson drag, and electromagnetic interactions. I began by validating numerical integration on simpler systems, including harmonic motion and convergence tests, before introducing the forces relevant to dust grains.

The electromagnetic model includes a stellar magnetic field and the effects associated with corotation. As a result, the radial and tangential components of a grain's velocity matter: the magnetic contribution changes as the grain moves relative to the rotating field. I track particle positions and velocities over time and use trajectory plots, radial time series, and derived quantities to study the resulting motion.

My analysis scripts organize simulation outputs into comparisons across physical parameters. In addition to the overall shape of an orbit, I examine radial turning points, oscillatory behavior, and timescales associated with the particle's cyclotron motion.

03 / RESEARCH

Experiments and parameter choices

I began with baseline runs that isolated gravity, radiation pressure, and drag, then varied the stellar magnetic field to examine the changes in orbital behavior. Subsequent experiments introduced field corotation and compared magnetic polarities, revealing that the orientation of the electromagnetic force can substantially change the direction of radial motion.

Grain size is another central variable. Charge-to-mass ratio changes strongly with particle size, making nanoscale grains particularly sensitive to electromagnetic forces. At the same time, radiation pressure on sufficiently small grains requires an appropriate grain-size-dependent radiation-pressure efficiency factor, Q_pr. I revisited the radiation model to account for this dependence and compared the updated trajectories against earlier runs. These comparisons helped identify which behaviors came from the underlying dynamics and which depended sensitively on model parameters.

04 / RESEARCH

Reading the trajectories

The orbital plots reveal several types of behavior: outward-moving grains, inward radial migration, and trajectories with pronounced epicyclic oscillations. Comparing the radial coordinate over time makes it possible to identify repeated turning points and distinguish bounded behavior from a transient oscillation during escape or migration.

The current phase of the project focuses on initial conditions that produce escape in the radiation-and-gravity baseline and asks how an increasingly strong magnetic field changes that outcome. I am using these experiments to study the transition toward bounded epicyclic motion and to compare the simulations with the physical picture in the literature.

05 / RESEARCH

My contribution

I built and refined the numerical workflow, implemented and checked force prescriptions, designed magnetic-field and grain-parameter experiments, and developed analysis tools for comparing simulation trajectories. An important part of the work has been revising physical assumptions when results exposed unexpected parameter sensitivities. That process continues to shape the next set of experiments.
