01 / RESEARCH

Research context

Cosmological simulations let researchers study galaxies in computational models of the evolving universe. They produce large datasets describing galaxies and their environments under different physical conditions. Examining those outputs also raises a computational question: how can a model learn the relationships within a high-dimensional simulation dataset and generate distributions that can be compared with the original samples?

I investigated this problem in a research tutorial with Professor Paul Torrey, working with data from the DREAMS cosmological simulation suite and conditional normalizing flows. The project brought together astrophysical data analysis, machine-learning experiments, and the practical challenges of working with research-scale computing resources.

02 / RESEARCH

Preparing data and adapting the workflow

I began by working with simulated galaxy properties and the existing Python research code. I prepared data for the modeling workflow and adapted code for conditional normalizing-flow experiments. A conditional normalizing flow learns a flexible probability distribution while incorporating information supplied as conditioning variables. That makes it useful for examining how a galaxy-property distribution changes across different settings represented in a dataset.

An important part of the work was connecting the scientific meaning of the simulation variables to the inputs and outputs of the machine-learning model. I organized the data and training workflow so that different experiments could be compared consistently.

03 / RESEARCH

Training and analysis on Rivanna

I ran experiments on UVA's Rivanna high-performance computing cluster, adjusting training settings and reviewing their effects on model outputs. Working on shared computing infrastructure required coordinating datasets, Python environments, experiment configurations, and saved results.

I then compared distributions generated by the model with those drawn from the cosmological simulations. The diagnostic plots provided a way to assess which features of the simulated data were represented by the model and where the outputs merited closer examination. This was also an exercise in interpreting machine-learning results in their scientific context: a plausible-looking distribution still has to be examined against the question that motivated the model.

04 / RESEARCH

What I learned from the project

This tutorial strengthened my ability to adapt an existing research codebase, manage computational experiments, and evaluate results from a generative model. It also deepened my interest in the ways computational methods can make complex scientific datasets easier to investigate. I became especially interested in the interfaces and visual explanations that allow researchers to move between the data, the model, and the questions they want to ask.

05 / RESEARCH

My contribution

I prepared simulation data, adapted Python code for conditional-flow experiments, ran training jobs on Rivanna, and analyzed generated-versus-simulated property distributions under Professor Torrey's supervision.
