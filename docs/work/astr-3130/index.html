01 / RESEARCH

From observation to measurement

In ASTR 3130, I worked through five laboratory investigations covering telescopes, detectors, imaging, stellar brightness, time-series data, and spectroscopy. The labs required moving from what an instrument recorded to measurements that could be interpreted and reported. Calibration, source selection, background estimation, and uncertainty analysis all affected the results.

The figures on this page come from collaborative ASTR 3130 course lab reports. They trace the measurements from telescope characterization and image reduction through variable-star photometry and spectroscopy.

02 / RESEARCH

I–II / Telescope measurements and limiting magnitude

Our first labs characterized the telescope's field of view and the relationships among aperture, exposure time, and the faintest objects visible in an observation. We measured stellar transit times and examined Pleiades exposures, then compared the observed trends with the relevant geometric and photometric expectations. In Lab 1, the transit timing was compared with the geometric 1/cos(δ) dependence.

03 / RESEARCH

III / Calibrating CCD images

We worked with FITS frames to examine detector signal and reduce astronomical images, including bias, dark, and flat-field corrections. Comparing raw and calibrated images made the instrumental effects visible. The lab also included measurements of image properties such as the apparent width of stellar sources and the detector's response under different conditions.

The image-processing workflow also involved identifying sources and examining how their light is distributed across pixels. Using tools such as Astropy and photutils, I explored automated stellar detection and measurements of the full width at half maximum (FWHM), a useful description of the apparent width of a stellar image.

04 / RESEARCH

IV / RR Lyrae variables in M3

Photometry turns a calibrated image into a measurement of light. I worked with aperture photometry, background estimation, and uncertainty propagation to estimate stellar brightness from image data. This required decisions about the source aperture, surrounding sky signal, and how measurement errors affect the result.

We used images of the globular cluster M3 to identify RR Lyrae variables and comparison stars. By measuring their brightness across an observing sequence and comparing the results with reference stars, we constructed light curves and examined their periodic behavior. Phase folding brought repeated cycles together, making the shape and timing of the variability easier to study.

05 / RESEARCH

V / Stellar spectroscopy

In the final lab, our group obtained optical spectra with a Shelyak LISA spectrograph mounted on a Celestron 14-inch telescope. We used Ne–Ar and He comparison lamps to map detector pixels to wavelengths, extracted one-dimensional spectra, and examined absorption features in stellar, lunar, and daytime-sky data.

I contributed to data reduction, wavelength calibration, and figure generation, with particular work on the daytime/twilight spectrum, unknown-star classification, lunar comparison, and the spectrograph's resolving-power measurement. I wrote the report's unknown-star classification analysis and helped prepare and check the figures. The analysis used absorption features including hydrogen Balmer lines and metal lines to compare unknown spectra with a reference stellar sequence; our four adopted classifications were G0, G5, K0, and K5. The measured resolving power for our instrument configuration was approximately R = 1857.

Our Lab 5 figures follow the reduction from lamp-line calibration and spectral extraction to a wavelength-calibrated unknown-star spectrum. We also compared reference stellar spectra across spectral classes and examined solar absorption features in the daytime sky and reflected moonlight.

06 / RESEARCH

What I took from the labs

These exercises gave me direct experience with the observational side of astronomy, including the measurement and calibration choices behind scientific plots. They also changed how I read results from simulations: observed quantities come with instrumental context, selection effects, and uncertainty that must be accounted for when comparing data with a model.
