01 / RESEARCH

Working with observational data

In ASTR 3130, I worked through five laboratory investigations covering astronomical imaging, photometry, time-series measurements, and spectroscopy. The course connected physical observations with their computational interpretation: a detector records pixel values or spectral signals, and those measurements need careful calibration, selection, and error analysis before they can answer a scientific question.

I used Python-based analysis tools alongside astronomical imaging software to process observational data and construct measurements. I became more familiar with the practical choices behind a result, including the treatment of instrumental effects, the selection of sources, and the uncertainty carried through each stage of analysis.

The figures on this page come from collaborative ASTR 3130 course lab reports. They trace the measurements from telescope characterization and image reduction through variable-star photometry and spectroscopy.

02 / RESEARCH

Telescope and detector characterization

Before analyzing astronomical targets, our group measured how the telescope and detector shaped what we could record. In Lab 1, we timed stellar transits across the field of view at different declinations and compared the measurements with the geometric dependence on 1/cos(δ).

Using Pleiades observations in Lab 2, we examined how limiting magnitude changed with aperture and exposure time. The plots make the tradeoffs between collecting area, integration time, and detectable faintness visible.

03 / RESEARCH

Calibrating CCD images

Astronomical images include instrumental signals alongside the light from celestial sources. I worked with FITS images and CCD calibration procedures, including bias, dark, and flat-field correction, to understand how raw images are prepared for quantitative analysis.

The image-processing workflow also involved identifying sources and examining how their light is distributed across pixels. Using tools such as Astropy and photutils, I explored automated stellar detection and measurements of the full width at half maximum (FWHM), a useful description of the apparent width of a stellar image.

The Lab 3 figures compare detector dark current across temperatures and show raw and reduced views of the M37 star field and galaxy M81.

04 / RESEARCH

RR Lyrae photometry in M3

Photometry turns a calibrated image into a measurement of light. I worked with aperture photometry, background estimation, and uncertainty propagation to estimate stellar brightness from image data. This required decisions about the source aperture, surrounding sky signal, and how measurement errors affect the result.

In Lab 4, our group marked RR Lyrae variables and comparison stars in a V-band image of the globular cluster M3. We calibrated brightness measurements over the observing sequence to construct light curves for four variables, then phase-folded the data using refined periods.

The calibrated and phase-folded views show how a time series can reveal repeating variation. They also make the effects of source selection, sky background, and measurement uncertainty easier to examine.

05 / RESEARCH

Stellar spectroscopy and classification

Spectroscopy introduced a different measurement process: relating position along a detector to wavelength, then examining the intensity and shape of spectral features. I worked with wavelength calibration and numerical fitting, including Gaussian models, to characterize features in spectral data.

Our Lab 5 figures follow the reduction from lamp-line calibration and spectral extraction to a wavelength-calibrated unknown-star spectrum. We also compared reference stellar spectra across spectral classes and examined solar absorption features in the daytime sky and reflected moonlight.

These analyses emphasized the importance of calibration and model assumptions. A measured peak, width, or line position only becomes physically informative when its observational and computational context is understood.

06 / RESEARCH

What I contributed and learned

Across these group laboratory projects, I used Python to organize astronomical data, carry out numerical measurements, fit models, estimate uncertainty, and make figures that connect the processed data to a physical interpretation. The experience gave me direct practice with the observational side of astronomy and with writing reproducible computational analyses of real measurements.

This course complements my simulation work: it taught me to think carefully about how the quantities used in a model are actually obtained from astronomical data, and how instrumentation and analysis decisions shape the evidence we use.
